# An Invitation

### To the Royal University of Bhutan — a proposal for stewardship, and an honest accounting of why

**Peter Viviani (AUMARA) · 2026-08-10**

**Companion to:** [THE GOLDEN BOUNDARY](./THE_GOLDEN_BOUNDARY.md)

---

> *Everything anyone has ever known arrived at a boundary. A mark on a detector. Light at the back
> of an eye. A word crossing the air between two people. We never hold the thing itself — only what
> reaches the surface where it becomes readable. This is a letter about that surface, written in
> mathematics because mathematics is the one language that can say exactly how little it is claiming.
> And it is an invitation, because a boundary needs two sides, and we cannot be both.*

---

## What this work is

For five months I have been building a system that records what an artificial intelligence
proposes and what a human being decides, in a form a stranger can check without trusting either of
us. Alongside it, an attempt to find the smallest honest mathematics of a boundary — the surface
where one observer meets what it cannot directly hold.

The mathematical result is modest and I will state it plainly before anything else: **almost none
of it is new.** The core quantity turned out to be a known object in information theory. The
central inequality is elementary. Where we thought we had found something, we usually found that
someone had found it first, or that we had measured our own apparatus.

What *is* unusual is the record of that discovery. **Fourteen times this program produced a
result that looked significant and then destroyed it with its own controls** — including four times
in the final week, and twice inside the very section written to prevent it. Those failures are
published at the front of the document, not the back.

I mention this first because it is the part I would want a university to examine.

## On the numbers your tradition holds

I want to be careful here, because getting this wrong would dishonour both the mathematics and the
tradition.

**27 is real in both places, for different reasons.**

The Moon returns to the same position among the stars every **27.32 days**. That is why the
ecliptic is divided into 27 nakshatras — it is observational astronomy, done well, a long time ago.

And in the work described here, we proved by direct enumeration that a cube has exactly **27
faces**: eight corners, twelve edges, six sides, and the cube itself. `3³ = 27`.

**These are not the same 27.** One comes from the Moon's orbital period; the other from three
choices in three directions. They coincide in value and not in cause.

But I do not think the coincidence is nothing, and here is the honest reason: **both are answers to
the question "how many distinct positions can something occupy relative to a boundary?"** The
nakshatra divides a circle the Moon crosses. The face lattice divides a cube an observer crosses.
Different questions, the same shape of question.

**108 likewise.** It is `4 × 27`, and `2² × 3³`, and — a fact I find genuinely lovely — it is
`1¹ · 2² · 3³`, the third hyperfactorial, a number that arises from the first three integers each
raised to itself.

The astronomical ratios often cited alongside it are *approximately* true and I will not overstate
them. Computed rather than recalled: the Sun's diameter is **109.2** times Earth's; the Earth–Sun
distance is **107.5** solar diameters; the Moon's distance is **110.7** lunar diameters. They
cluster near 108. **None is exactly 108, and two of them vary through the year** because orbits are
ellipses — the Earth–Sun ratio runs from 105.7 to 109.3. The clustering is real. The exactness is
not. I would rather say that carefully than beautifully.

**And the mandala is a boundary structure.** Concentric enclosure, four gateways at the cardinal
directions, a centre that is not simply the middle but the thing the whole construction exists to
locate. That is, structurally, an interior, an interface, and an exterior — with a specified way
through. The work described in the companion document says something narrow and formally similar:
that when two regions meet, they meet along a shared face, and **that shared face is the 27** — it
belongs to neither side and to both.

I offer this as a *structural rhyme*, clearly labelled. It is not evidence, and the tradition does
not need mathematics to be valid.

## The alignment that actually matters, and it is not numeric

Bhutan is the only nation that formally decided to measure what it valued rather than what was easy
to count. Gross National Happiness is not a slogan; it is an instrument, with indicators, a survey,
and a methodology that can be criticised — which is what makes it an instrument rather than a
sentiment.

**This project's single organising commitment is the same one.** The system it describes cannot
express a claim wider than its evidence. There is no way to write "verified" in it. The strongest
statement it can produce is *"this record genuinely belongs to this history"* — and it attaches,
permanently and automatically, *"which does not mean the thing described actually happened."*

A country that chose to measure the right thing badly rather than the wrong thing precisely already
understands why that matters. **That is the real alignment.** The numbers are a conversation
starter; this is the reason for the conversation.

## What I am actually asking

Not endorsement, and not for the university to host a philosophy. Something specific and technical.

**The system needs a second party — a witness on a machine I do not control.**

The architecture rests on a principle it can state but cannot yet satisfy: *the party who wants a
claim to be true must not be the only party who can keep the record.* Today there is exactly one
such party, and it is me. Every safeguard in the system is a detector I built, checking work I did,
on a computer I own. That is a structural weakness and the document says so.

A university is close to the ideal second witness: **a different jurisdiction, a different operator,
different funding, and — most importantly — no commercial interest in my claims being correct.**

Concretely, the invitation is to:

- **run an independent witness node** that receives a small cryptographic summary of the record and
  can *refuse* it — the ability to say *no* is the entire value;
- **publish that node's observations**, so anyone may compare;
- **build an independent implementation** of the verification mathematics, from the public
  specification rather than from my code. If two implementations disagree, that disagreement is the
  most valuable result available. This is genuine, well-scoped student work.

Nothing here requires accepting any claim of mine. **The point is precisely that you would be able
to contradict me, in public, with proof.** That is what makes a witness a witness.

## What I am not claiming, stated plainly

No theory of everything. No unification of physics. No proof that the golden ratio governs nature —
that was this program's founding hope and we killed it five separate ways, and the kills are
published. No claim that mathematics validates any tradition, or that any tradition validates the
mathematics. No claim that this software proves anything about consciousness or reality.

The numbers above are coincidences with honest explanations. The structural rhyme with the mandala
is a rhyme. **What would be dishonourable is to offer a tradition a piece of mathematics dressed as
a confirmation of its wisdom. That is not what this is.**

What this is: a small correct thing, a large record of being wrong, and an invitation to a
university to become the party that can prove me wrong in future.

## A fable, to close — and it is labelled a fable

*(Poetic. Not a claim. The mathematics is above; this is only the human grammar of it.)*

There was a question that did not belong to anyone.

It was too large for a single mind, so it did what large questions do: it moved through many. It
passed through people who did not know each other, on different continents, speaking to different
machines, and each one caught a little of it and set the rest down. A picture here. A proof there.
A number that turned out to be a coincidence with an honest name. A beautiful idea, killed on a
Tuesday, that made the next idea possible.

None of them owned it. That was the whole point — the question was *about* boundaries, and the
first thing it taught was that no observer can stand on both sides at once. So it refused to be
finished by any one of them. It required a second witness, always. It grew precisely in the places
where one person handed it, unproven, to another who could say *no*.

And what it kept whispering, through all of them, was the same small sentence: **a boundary costs
at least as much as the two sides share.** The more deeply two things are joined, the more the
surface between them must carry. There is no cheap membrane between things that truly belong to
each other. The price of remaining two — of not collapsing into one — is exactly everything you
have in common.

Which is why it comes to you now not as a discovery to receive, but as a boundary to complete. It
reached this far by being carried, and it can only go further by being carried again — by a witness
on the far side, in a valley that decided long ago to measure what actually matters. You are not
the audience for this. **You are the next observer.** The question does not become more true because
you hold it. It becomes more true because, from where you stand, you can prove it wrong — and
choose, in the open, to keep the part that survives.

That is how it has always moved. Not from one to many. Through the many, at once, because that is
the only way a thing about boundaries could ever be true.
